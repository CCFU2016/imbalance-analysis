Profile-Specific Insights

  • Solar producers face the highest imbalance risk by far: in 2024, 18.5% of days delivered a negative return, vs 10.1% for baseload. On day-ahead the same year shows only 2.3% negative days for solar — an 8× reduction in risk by switching markets.
  • The structural risk gap widens with profile concentration: solar is concentrated in midday hours when imbalance prices crash hardest due to grid surplus. Day-ahead is more orderly because prices are set the day before based on forecast supply/demand.
  • Consumer worst-case periods: short windows (24h) show imbalance much more expensive, but the gap shrinks with window length and reverses at 14d/21d in some years — short spikes dominate the imbalance premium.
  • For all four profiles, day-ahead has dramatically lower probability of unfavourable periods. The largest gap is solar 2024: imbalance 18.5% vs day-ahead 2.3% on a 24h basis.
  • Day-ahead 14-day risk is essentially zero across all 4 profiles for all 5 years. Imbalance only crosses zero at 14d for solar in 2024 (0.8%).

When the Dutch grid requires both upward and downward regulation simultaneously — the spread between Shortage and Surplus prices

What is Regulation State 2?

State -1 (DOWN): Grid has surplus, only downward regulation activated
State 0 (STABLE): No regulation needed
State 1 (UP): Grid has shortage, only upward regulation activated
State 2 (UP_AND_DOWN): Both upward AND downward regulation activated simultaneously — creating a spread between Shortage and Surplus prices

Why does it matter?

During State 2, Price Shortage ≠ Price Surplus. Consumers (short positions) pay the higher Shortage price while producers (long positions) receive the lower Surplus price. The spread is the cost of being on the wrong side. Two 1 MW assets on opposite sides (one long, one short) together pay this spread — it is a pure cost with no net energy position.

Total Annual Spread: Two 1 MW Assets on Opposite Sides of Imbalance — NL vs FR

If you operate two 1 MW assets — one always long (receives the positive imbalance price) and one always short (pays the negative imbalance price) — the combined spread cost = Σ|positive − negative| × interval length. NL settles in 15-min ISPs (TenneT Price Surplus / Price Shortage); FR settles in 30-min intervals (RTE Prix des Ecarts Positifs / Négatifs). Full calendar-year totals, 2021–2025.

Over the full 5-year window NL accumulated ~€300k more in spread cost (€745k vs €446k, +67%) — the Dutch imbalance market is structurally fatter-tailed.
NL median is 0.00 every year because TenneT runs a single-price regime outside State 2 (Surplus = Shortage); FR prices are almost always asymmetric, so even the median spread is non-zero. FR values account for RTE's switch from 30-minute to 15-minute settlement intervals on 2025-01-01.

Key Takeaways

  • State 2 (dual regulation) has tripled from 8% of the year (2021) to 27% (2025). Stable periods (no regulation) have nearly disappeared: 12.7% → 1.3%.
  • The annual spread cost for two 1 MW assets on opposite sides ranges from 63k (2021) to 264k EUR (2024). This is a pure friction cost with no net energy position — it arises entirely from the Shortage/Surplus price gap during State 2.
  • The average spread per MWh during State 2 is 82–179 EUR/MWh, but the distribution is heavily skewed — the median (40–110 EUR/MWh) is roughly half the average, with extreme outliers up to 3,940 EUR/MWh.
  • 2024 had the highest total spread cost (264k EUR) despite having fewer State 2 hours than 2025 — because the average spread per MWh was much higher (179 vs 82). The 2022 energy crisis also produced a high average spread (176 EUR/MWh).
  • Trend: State 2 is becoming more frequent but with a lower average spread per event. This suggests more routine dual regulation (possibly from renewable variability) rather than crisis-driven events.
